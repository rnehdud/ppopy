## [safety_method] Estimating systemic exposure from dermal use
- source_url: https://op.europa.eu/en/publication-detail/-/publication/0393df6a-4e1f-11ef-acbc-01aa75ed71a1/language-en
- locator: SCCS/1647/22, 12th revision, corrigendum 2, PDF p. 47, printed p. 43, section 3-3.5.4, equations 4–5
- checked_on: 2026-11-26
- scope: non_clinical_only
- limitations: This describes a specified historical guidance edition, not an individual exposure estimate or a statement that no newer guidance exists. Product concentration, actual use and an appropriately measured absorption value are needed for a scenario-specific estimate. Independently worded and adapted from the European Union SCCS 12th-revision guidance.

The SCCS 12th-revision guidance describes systemic exposure per unit of body weight using the amount of an ingredient absorbed per skin area, the treated area and application frequency. Its example equation uses a default body weight of 60 kg, which is an assessment assumption rather than an individual's measured weight. When absorption is expressed as a percentage, the calculation combines estimated daily product exposure per unit of body weight, the ingredient concentration in the finished product and the percentage absorbed. That product-exposure term already incorporates the amount applied and application frequency, so frequency should not be multiplied a second time. The guidance says in vitro study doses and concentrations should mimic intended use without exceeding it; otherwise, penetration can be underestimated. If actual application frequency differs from the standard frequency used for a default exposure value, the estimate must be adjusted accordingly.

## [safety_method] Point of departure and margin of safety
- source_url: https://op.europa.eu/en/publication-detail/-/publication/0393df6a-4e1f-11ef-acbc-01aa75ed71a1/language-en
- locator: SCCS/1647/22, 12th revision, corrigendum 2, PDF pp. 16 and 91, printed pp. 12 and 87, sections 3-1 and 3-5.1–3-5.1.1.2
- checked_on: 2026-11-27
- scope: non_clinical_only
- limitations: This independently worded summary describes a specified historical guidance edition, not a current legal standard or an individual safety verdict. The point of departure and exposure estimate concern the endpoint and scenario assessed and do not on their own address every local effect or establish that later guidance is absent.

The SCCS 12th-revision guidance defines a point of departure as a point on the dose–response relationship used to begin low-dose extrapolation for substances with or without a threshold. It defines the margin of safety as the systemic point of departure divided by the estimated systemic exposure dose. Repeated-dose oral studies are often used as substitutes when dermal exposure studies are unavailable, while a dermal systemic exposure estimate is commonly used for comparison. Where usable in vivo data are available, the guidance prefers the benchmark-dose approach, and it also states that determining a benchmark dose is not limited to in vivo data. The guidance uses a benchmark dose as the systemic point of departure where possible; historical no-observed-adverse-effect levels are usually used when a benchmark dose cannot be calculated. Evaluating the underlying data also requires attention to the nature and severity of effects and their dose–response relationships.

## [safety_method] Inhalation exposure from sprays and powders
- source_url: https://op.europa.eu/en/publication-detail/-/publication/0393df6a-4e1f-11ef-acbc-01aa75ed71a1/language-en
- locator: SCCS/1647/22, 12th revision, corrigendum 2, PDF pp. 26 and 34, printed pp. 22 and 30, sections 3-3.4.1.3 and 3-3.4.2.3
- checked_on: 2026-11-26
- scope: non_clinical_only
- limitations: This describes the specified guidance edition rather than the actual exposure or safety of a particular product. The relevant particle characteristics and exposure conditions depend on the finished product and use setting; later guidance is not ruled out. Independently worded and adapted from the European Union SCCS 12th-revision guidance.

The SCCS 12th-revision guidance describes inhalation exposure from evaporation, sprays and powders. Evaporation is relevant to volatile substances, while a spray or powder can also introduce nonvolatile substances into the air as droplets or particles. Assessing these products involves particle or droplet size distribution and deposition in different parts of the respiratory tract. Shape, density and other particle properties can also influence deposition, while formulation and spray-device characteristics can influence droplet size. For estimating spray exposure, the guidance distinguishes the concentration in inhalable mist from that in the original formulation.

## [safety_method] Characterising nanomaterials during testing
- source_url: https://op.europa.eu/en/publication-detail/-/publication/0393df6a-4e1f-11ef-acbc-01aa75ed71a1/language-en
- locator: SCCS/1647/22, 12th revision, corrigendum 2, PDF pp. 113–114, printed pp. 109–110, sections 3-6.8.2–3-6.8.3
- checked_on: 2026-11-26
- scope: non_clinical_only
- limitations: These are testing considerations from the specified edition, not a general safety conclusion about a named nanomaterial or product. The material, coating, exposure route and intended product must be identified separately. Independently worded and adapted from the European Union SCCS 12th-revision guidance.

The SCCS 12th-revision guidance notes that nanoparticles can agglomerate or bind other materials at their surfaces, and that their behaviour can change in the presence of stabilising or dispersing agents. Characterising a nanomaterial before and during a test helps establish what material the test system was exposed to. Agglomeration, settling or binding in the test medium can change exposure and affect interpretation of results. Surface modification or coating may change physicochemical properties and potentially toxic effects. The guidance discusses information about both coating materials and the modified nanomaterial, including stability and penetration where relevant.

## [safety_method] Assessing uncertainty in PBPK models
- source_url: https://op.europa.eu/en/publication-detail/-/publication/0393df6a-4e1f-11ef-acbc-01aa75ed71a1/language-en
- locator: SCCS/1647/22, 12th revision, corrigendum 2, PDF pp. 45–46, printed pp. 41–42, section 3-3.5.3
- checked_on: 2026-11-26
- scope: non_clinical_only
- limitations: This describes the cited guidance edition, not an assessment of a specific model or proof that a prediction is suitable for an individual consumer decision. Later guidance and a specific model's evidence require separate review. Independently worded and adapted from the European Union SCCS 12th-revision guidance.

In the SCCS 12th-revision guidance, uncertainty analysis examines how incomplete knowledge of model parameters or structure affects predicted dose metrics. The guidance distinguishes uncertainty about parameter values from variability across a population, and explains how sensitivity and uncertainty can affect confidence in predictions. For quantitative risk assessment it requests sufficient information to evaluate model structure, parameters, implementation, simulations and validation. For predictions intended for margin-of-safety calculations, it also asks for evidence that the model predicts experimental data not used to construct it. If insufficient information is supplied to evaluate the calculations, the guidance says model results may be used only as supporting information.

## [safety_method] Aggregate exposure across products and routes
- source_url: https://op.europa.eu/en/publication-detail/-/publication/0393df6a-4e1f-11ef-acbc-01aa75ed71a1/language-en
- locator: SCCS/1647/22, 12th revision, corrigendum 2, PDF pp. 16 and 34, printed pp. 12 and 30, sections 3-1 and 3-3.4.3
- checked_on: 2026-11-26
- scope: non_clinical_only
- limitations: This describes the specified historical guidance edition, not an individual's exposure calculation or a claim that later guidance does not exist. The products, routes and scenario must be identified before the method is applied. Independently worded and adapted from the European Union SCCS 12th-revision guidance.

The SCCS 12th-revision guidance describes aggregate exposure as the sum of relevant exposures to an ingredient from the product categories and routes being considered. For local exposure, the aggregation is specific to the body site under investigation, while systemic exposure assessment must consider the product categories contributing through a given route. The guidance says that combining exposure across routes, when necessary, is calculated at the internal exposure level and should be conservative. It also notes that exposure of vulnerable consumer groups, including children or pregnant people, may need separate consideration. In general, the guidance focuses on cosmetic uses but describes an exception for carcinogenic, mutagenic or reproductive toxicants, for which non-cosmetic uses should also be considered.

## [safety_method] Local and systemic exposure are assessed separately
- source_url: https://op.europa.eu/en/publication-detail/-/publication/0393df6a-4e1f-11ef-acbc-01aa75ed71a1/language-en
- locator: SCCS/1647/22, 12th revision, corrigendum 2, PDF p. 23, printed p. 19, sections 3-3.3–3-3.3.2
- checked_on: 2026-11-26
- scope: non_clinical_only
- limitations: The paragraph explains distinctions in the specified guidance edition and does not calculate exposure, diagnose a reaction or determine the safety of a product. A relevant effect, body site and exposure scenario must be assessed individually. Independently worded and adapted from the European Union SCCS 12th-revision guidance.

The SCCS 12th-revision guidance distinguishes local effects from systemic effects when identifying the exposure measure needed for assessment. Local effects may depend on the amount of a substance contacting tissues at a particular body site and are considered using a local external dose. The guidance identifies skin, the respiratory tract and the mouth as important exposure entrances, with eyes or other sites relevant to selected products. Systemic exposure instead depends on uptake via the relevant route and can be estimated as a systemic exposure dose for comparison with toxicological evidence. A systemic estimate by itself does not establish whether a local effect will occur.

## [safety_method] Screening and refinement in exposure assessment
- source_url: https://op.europa.eu/en/publication-detail/-/publication/0393df6a-4e1f-11ef-acbc-01aa75ed71a1/language-en
- locator: SCCS/1647/22, 12th revision, corrigendum 2, PDF p. 24, printed p. 20, section 3-3.4.1
- checked_on: 2026-11-26
- scope: non_clinical_only
- limitations: This is a description of a specified historical guidance edition, not an individual exposure estimate, product safety verdict, or assertion that no later guidance exists. Independently worded and adapted from the European Union SCCS guidance.

The SCCS 12th-revision guidance describes a staged approach to estimating exposure from cosmetic ingredients. An initial screening can use general exposure scenarios and conservative fixed input values, including a deterministic calculation of aggregate exposure. Where refinement is needed, the assessment can use a justified probabilistic model or another appropriate method. For regulatory use, the guidance expects a probabilistic approach to be conservative, realistic and transparent. These assessment stages do not themselves determine a particular person's exposure or a product's safety.

## [safety_method] Assumptions in probabilistic cosmetic exposure assessments
- source_url: https://op.europa.eu/en/publication-detail/-/publication/0393df6a-4e1f-11ef-acbc-01aa75ed71a1/language-en
- locator: SCCS/1647/22, 12th revision, corrigendum 2, PDF p. 24, printed p. 20, section 3-3.4.1, recommendations 2–5
- checked_on: 2026-11-26
- scope: non_clinical_only
- limitations: This describes modelling assumptions in the named historical SCCS guidance, not observed frequencies, individual exposure values, a product rule or a claim that later guidance does not exist. Independently worded and adapted from the European Union SCCS guidance.

For a probabilistic assessment of systemic exposure used to derive a margin of safety, the SCCS 12th-revision guidance identifies the 95th percentile of the assessed European population's exposure as the target. It normally calls for ingredient concentrations that cover a worst case and assumes the ingredient occurs in every product category where its use is allowed. The guidance describes the latter as a conservative modelling assumption because product use can be dependent and occurrence can change over time; a different occurrence probability needs specific supporting data. Model equations and input parameters should accompany exposure estimates so that calculations can be reproduced. These assumptions are not measurements showing that every product actually contains the ingredient.

## [safety_method] Dose schedule and assessment factors in the 12th revision
- source_url: https://op.europa.eu/en/publication-detail/-/publication/0393df6a-4e1f-11ef-acbc-01aa75ed71a1/language-en
- locator: SCCS/1647/22, 12th revision, corrigendum 2, PDF p. 92, printed p. 88, section 3-5.1.1.4
- checked_on: 2026-11-26
- scope: non_clinical_only
- limitations: This independently worded summary describes one specified historical guidance revision; it does not establish the current method, a legal limit or safety for a particular ingredient or person. Applicability depends on the original study, endpoint and expert assessment.

The SCCS 12th revision explains that the point of departure may need adjustment when a repeated-dose study does not use a daily dosing schedule. For its example of dosing on five days per week, the guidance describes multiplying the point of departure by 5/7 for a margin-of-safety calculation. When the point of departure is based on a lowest observed adverse effect level, it describes an additional assessment factor often set at three, with a higher factor considered case by case according to the study and effects; some studies may be unsuitable. If a 90-day repeated-dose study is unavailable and a NOAEL or BMDL from a 28-day repeated-dose study is used, the document describes a default factor of three for exposure duration. These are context-dependent steps in that guidance, not values that can be assigned to a product without its original toxicology data and exposure assessment.

## [safety_method] Benchmark dose modelling and uncertainty
- source_url: https://op.europa.eu/en/publication-detail/-/publication/0393df6a-4e1f-11ef-acbc-01aa75ed71a1/language-en
- locator: SCCS/1647/22, 12th revision, corrigendum 2, PDF pp. 91–92, printed pp. 87–88, sections 3-5.1.1.2–3-5.1.1.3
- checked_on: 2026-11-26
- scope: non_clinical_only
- limitations: This independently worded summary describes a specified historical guidance revision and not a current legal or product-safety determination. Model selection and response assumptions require evaluation of the actual toxicological data.

The SCCS 12th revision describes benchmark-dose analysis as fitting a dose–response model to available data and using it to estimate a dose associated with a defined response. It distinguishes the resulting estimate from its lower and upper confidence limits and describes using the upper-to-lower limit ratio as an indication of uncertainty. The document notes that different models applied to the same data can produce different benchmark-dose estimates and lower limits. It also states that choosing and applying a model requires expert judgement and modelling expertise. A modelled dose is therefore tied to its endpoint, data and assumptions rather than a universal ingredient concentration limit.

## [safety_method] Oral study data and systemic point of departure
- source_url: https://op.europa.eu/en/publication-detail/-/publication/0393df6a-4e1f-11ef-acbc-01aa75ed71a1/language-en
- locator: SCCS/1647/22, 12th revision, corrigendum 2, PDF pp. 92–93, printed pp. 88–89, section 3-5.1.2
- checked_on: 2026-11-26
- scope: non_clinical_only
- limitations: This independently worded summary describes the specified historical guidance and does not determine an ingredient's absorption or an individual's exposure. Route extrapolation and defaults require assessment of the available substance-specific information.

The SCCS 12th revision discusses how oral absorption affects the use of an oral study to derive a systemic point of departure. It explains that an external oral dose and the corresponding internal dose are the same only when oral absorption is complete. In the absence of oral absorption data, the document describes a default assumption of 50% and a corresponding division of the oral point of departure by two; where information suggests poor oral bioavailability, it says a 10% default could be considered. Available oral absorption and other kinetic data should be used or considered, and the oral 50% default must be distinguished from the guidance's separate dermal default. First-pass metabolism and the relevant target organ can also make route-to-route extrapolation unsuitable. These assumptions cannot establish a particular ingredient's actual absorption or substitute for its own exposure and toxicology evidence.

## [safety_method] Conditions that influence an in vitro dermal absorption study
- source_url: https://op.europa.eu/en/publication-detail/-/publication/0393df6a-4e1f-11ef-acbc-01aa75ed71a1/language-en
- locator: SCCS/1647/22, 12th revision, corrigendum 2, PDF pp. 37–38, printed pp. 33–34, section 3-3.5.1.1
- checked_on: 2026-11-26
- scope: non_clinical_only
- limitations: This independently worded summary describes a specified historical guidance revision and does not establish an ingredient's absorption rate or an individual's skin condition. Study suitability depends on the actual material, design and intended product use.

The SCCS 12th revision describes dermal absorption as depending on the substance, the skin and the conditions under which the product is applied. Its examples include the substance's properties, body site, exposure duration, concentration, vehicle and skin integrity. For in vitro studies, it advises checking the integrity of the skin preparation and characterising the test substance so that it corresponds to the intended cosmetic ingredient. It also says the applied dose, formulation and contact time should represent intended use and that the analytical method's sensitivity and limits should be documented. An absorption result should therefore be interpreted with the preparation and study conditions rather than transferred automatically to another product or person.

## [safety_method] Missing dermal absorption data and dose interpretation
- source_url: https://op.europa.eu/en/publication-detail/-/publication/0393df6a-4e1f-11ef-acbc-01aa75ed71a1/language-en
- locator: SCCS/1647/22, 12th revision, corrigendum 2, PDF p. 39, printed p. 35, section 3-3.5.1.1
- checked_on: 2026-11-26
- scope: non_clinical_only
- limitations: The default and study-design discussion belong to the specified historical guidance and are not a measurement for a particular ingredient. Actual test data, formulation and exposure scenario require separate assessment.

The SCCS 12th revision discusses a default dermal absorption value of 50% when experimentally determined absorption is unavailable and notes that it may also be used when available data are inadequate. The same section cautions that an in vitro applied amount exceeding intended use can cause systemic exposure to be underestimated if absorption expressed as a percentage of that test dose is used without considering the conditions. It also calls for attention to formulation effects on a compound's availability. These are assessment assumptions and study-design concerns, not evidence that every ingredient penetrates skin at the default rate. A product-specific exposure estimate needs suitable substance, formulation and use information.

## [safety_method] Defining a reasonably foreseeable exposure scenario
- source_url: https://op.europa.eu/en/publication-detail/-/publication/0393df6a-4e1f-11ef-acbc-01aa75ed71a1/language-en
- locator: SCCS/1647/22, 12th revision, corrigendum 2, PDF pp. 22–23, printed pp. 18–19, section 3-3.2
- checked_on: 2026-11-27
- scope: non_clinical_only
- limitations: This independently worded summary describes a specified historical guidance edition, not a measured exposure, product safety verdict or confirmation that no later guidance exists. Actual product and use data must be identified before constructing a specific scenario.

The SCCS 12th-revision guidance describes exposure scenarios in terms of reasonably foreseeable conditions of use. Its non-exhaustive list includes the product type and application method, whether the product is rinsed off or left on, the ingredient concentration and the quantity applied each time. It also identifies frequency, contact area, exposure duration and, when specifically needed, the intended consumer group. Sun exposure of the treated area, indoor or outdoor use and ventilation are additional relevant conditions in the guidance. The appropriate dermal, oral and inhalation routes depend on the intended product and its use; missing product-specific values are not measurements of an individual person's exposure.

## [safety_method] FDA review of glycolic acid and ultraviolet response
- source_url: https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/guidance-industry-labeling-cosmetics-containing-alpha-hydroxy-acids
- locator: FDA Guidance for Industry, January 2005, Section IV: Safety Reviews of AHAs
- checked_on: 2026-11-27
- scope: non_clinical_only
- limitations: This describes FDA's 2005 review of selected studies, not a legal concentration limit or a prediction for a particular product or person. The reviewed glycolic-acid results cannot automatically establish the effects of every AHA, formulation or exposure schedule.

In its 2005 AHA labelling guidance, FDA described its review of six human studies on ultraviolet responses following topical AHA exposure. The studies assessed markers including sunburn cells and the minimum ultraviolet dose producing visible redness, and one study assessed a marker of DNA damage. FDA reported that studies of glycolic acid found altered ultraviolet responses at concentrations as low as 4% and durations as short as four days. These values describe conditions represented in the reviewed evidence; they are not a universal threshold below which every product is risk free. FDA also observed differences in responsiveness between participants and stated that the available studies did not identify how to predict who would be more sensitive. The review concerned the studied conditions and short-term exposures, so it cannot establish the response to an unidentified formulation or all longer-term outcomes.
